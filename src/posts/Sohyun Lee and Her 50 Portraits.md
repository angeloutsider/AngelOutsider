<p>My first resonant impression of Sohyun Lee was when she deftly snapped a photo of me eating a breakfast sandwich outside of Proof Bakery. The granular feeling of English muffin crumbs lingered on my face even after I swiped them away in vain. In this capture that would’ve otherwise felt sudden for two people having just met for the first time, I felt an immediate sense of warmth and familiarity rather than awkward intrusion.</p>

<p>I met Sohyun (fortunately) by sliding into her DMs (unfortunately). In my personal journey towards soundness and security within my mind and body, I pushed myself to pursue modeling and sought out collaborative test shoots. By chance, I came across her account and was immediately pulled in by her images that carried the same warmth that was confirmed when I met her in person. Honest and unfolding, her work emerged as an archive and portal into the intimacies of daily life and the formation of self.</p>

<p>What I further gauged from both admiring and working with Sohyun was how she found simple truth in the “as is.” Although we agreed to first meet up at Proof to get to know one another before shooting, Sohyun still suggested we do a few shots around the block. With my chin laden with crumbs and a pimple patch very much full and intact, I was nevertheless comfortable enough to accept and stand alongside the vibrantly colored walls of Atwater Village.</p>

<p>Sohyun liked everything I would typically delete from my photo library. A yawn, a squeaky blink (due to my very dry eyes), and my hands instinctively reaching to scratch an itch were points of tension that would excite her. More significantly, they were points of tension that treaded between discomfort and domesticity. I began to note these reactions as distinctive responses of my body. They were entirely unique, tailored to my bodily needs, and therefore proved my existence and individual personhood.</p>

<p>These themes became ever more clear when Sohyun invited me to participate in her upcoming project 50 Portraits | No one comes from themselves. The project, rooted in the fundamental understanding that human life is multidimensional, positions 50 portraits in conversation with the stories of their subject. She does not seek profundity, but rather aims to use this project as an opportunity to simply meet people she would otherwise find it unlikely to cross paths with.</p>

<p>It was only fitting that we shot on Mulholland Drive and Ventura Blvd, two locations that were both deeply personal and intuitive to my becoming. Mulholland Drive was a place I went to dream, to weep, and to feel insignificant. Ventura Blvd was home to my parents’ former restaurant and the default meet up spot I frequented with all my childhood friends. Simultaneously, it was also a physical marker of a rapidly changing city landscape as the stores I treasured growing up had since been lost due to increasing gentrification.</p>

<p>Followed by the portrait session, Sohyun provided thoughtful questions, ironically difficult in their simplicity, to create holistic understandings of how each person in her 50 different portraits came to be. Through dynamics of family, culture, place, history, relationships, labor, chance, and circumstance, Sohyun renders visible impulse and our habitual modes of adaptation rather than seeking an “impressive story or a tidy ending.”</p>

<p>Although no single background or explanation can ever fully represent a person in their full dimension, the traits we inherit from family, culture, and even luck inevitably shape who we are. In Sohyun's own words, no one is formed alone.</p>
