<p>Lately, I have begun to feel suffocated by the ever tightening grip of AI, algorithm, and automation upon every facet of my life. The content I consume is delivered to me via algorithm and more and more frequently does that content reveal itself to be AI generated. It has come to the point where I have lost my belief in reality. I watch TV ad testimonials and notice oddities in the way the people move their mouths, taking it as proof of their digital nature. The visual aids for the menu at the restaurant are AI generated “drawings” of the food, rendering it entirely meaningless for the purposes of gauging what the food will look like. Every time I would like to search something up on google, I am given an ai overview, meaning I will never have to look below a top result to find all that I desire.</p>

<p>Suddenly, almost unbeknownst to me, I have gone from someone who never used AI to someone who has begun to use it on a daily basis. This has weighed upon my spirit as I ruminate on the environmental impact, the undeniable deterioration of my critical thinking skills, and of course, the approximately 10% chance that it will have the power to wipe us all out by the end of the decade.</p>

<p>While these elements have made me question my future, my purpose, and my point in living, I have decided such hopeless deliberations are wholly unproductive. Instead, I have decided to take a practical approach to feel the warmth of a human hand in various sectors of my life once more.</p>

<p>Here are some things I have started doing to ensure that AI and algorithms do not become the center of my world. Please also welcome additions from Amelie (notated in pink) as we navigate our first joint article. The following are our thoughts in conversation.</p>

<h1>1. Maybe Radio Might Save Us</h1>

<p>I think my greatest saving grace right now is human curation, especially in music. I’m an apple music user so I’ve never been all that algorithmically reliant in finding new music, however, radio has been a much needed reminder that there are people in the world who search and listen and love and wish to share their discoveries with me. From a practical perspective as well, listening to independent radio stations have helped to expand my music taste and make me feel like I am made of flesh and blood again.</p>

<p>Radio is magical because it’s listening to someone across the world play a song that they love so much that they would like to share it with someone else at this very moment. On a day they are glum, they choose songs that reflect that and perhaps I am glum too or perhaps I am just bearing witness to the feelings and desires of this stranger. There is something very necessary about relinquishing some of our choice in matters of content. With everything becoming hyper tailored to a manufactured sense of individual preference based on user behavior and surveillance, appreciating someone else’s taste in art is a necessary and undervalued act of delayed gratification.</p>

<p>In a very physical sense as well, listening to the radio has made the humming and buzzing in my head from all the digital drivel quiet slightly as my brain feels a, frequently elusive, sense of calm. I currently listen to NTS the most but there’s so many beautiful radio stations all around and I invite you, dear reader, to dive into the beautiful world of independent radio. Some great LA based radio stations are KCRW, KXLU, and dublab.</p>

<h1 style="color: #F2928E">2. Welcome slowness and think about the source</h1>

<p style="color: #F2928E">On the note of radio, I’ve recently spurred efforts to ingest media that is both delivered slowly and processed slowly. Following my departure from Spotify (due to their investment in AI military weaponry), I was in search of something that would both challenge and satisfy me with the comfort of sound. I stepped into the world of audiobooks with my first listen being Braiding Sweetgrass by Robin Wall Kimmerer, who dives into Indigenous teachings of gratitude, reciprocity, and honor with the Earth (more on this to come*). At first, I found it hard to retain information and stay engaged. My ears were fidgety and my hands wandered aimlessly, unable to grasp at the familiar paper bounds that would typically be present. Slowness was unwelcome in my mind. I was so used to having music at a finger's touch, unlimited skips, and favorable choices deftly assembled by the algorithm. I found my first moments of true immersion on the train to work and during endless traffic-hour drives. As I adjusted to the voice of the author reading her own words back to me, I found myself envisioning her descriptions in my mind as if they emerged from my own memory. There was something so nourishing, so intimate about this form of oral history and the feeling of being a toddler again with your loved one reading to you because you couldn’t yet make out the syllables yourself.</p>

<p style="color: #F2928E">As part of her lessons on honoring the earth, Kimmerer calls us to examine the life and animation of the natural world we have long objectified––a similar process rooted in slowness and intention. A simple drinking glass was once sand, pummeled and weathered into fine grains over the course of millions of years. The junk mail we easily toss upon delivery was once a wise tree. As we examine the source, think about where your music, sound, or entertainment might be coming from.</p>

<h1>3. Placing the Choice Back into Our Hands</h1>

<p>The cruelty of the current digital landscape is that AI has now become the default. To undo this automation, I have changed my default browser to Duck Duck Go which gives users the choice between a regular search engine and an ai browser (I found out about this through NPR which I've been listening to on Apple Music). While I still need to use google sometimes (for google translate mostly), this small change has made me more aware of when I am using AI and also adds a tad more friction to the retrieval of information. While the difference may be miniscule, I would say this is a small step towards maintaining my autonomy and inviting the human hand back into my online experience as i read a little list or article written by some netizen as opposed to a list of easily consumable bullet points dredged up from the interwebs.</p>

<h1>4. Trying to be bored more</h1>

<p>I vividly remember leaving T2-3D: Battle Across Time, a ride at Universal Studios circa 2010  based on the hit movie franchise Terminator, in tears and cold sweats as I imagined a world in which we have forsaken our humanity in pursuit of digital evolution and created our demise in the process. I currently teach English to students in Korea, many of who work in an impacted field and laugh about how their company fired 70% of their staff but they just have to find a way to make themselves indispensable. Other students admit that they feel scared about the state of AI but feel opposition is futile and will ultimately hurt their career prospects. As someone who loves and cherishes the human hand and human spirit in everything, the ever increasing horrors feels like the end.</p>

<p>Truthfully, every new thing I hear on the news on various fronts makes me question why I am alive, however, I’ve decided that what will save me from succumbing into a deep dark hole of despair is boredom.</p>

<p>I have become a sad little mouse in a cage of my own making with a lever that I pull whenever I need dopamine. And no matter how much I convince myself that the meager scraps of fake cheese that i get when i pull this lever are feeding me or at least making me feel better in some way, I must realize that all the fake cheese (be it short form or long form) makes my stomach hurt and doesn’t even taste good. Instead, I must unlock my little cage and go for a walk without my phone, sit on the train and stare off into space with nothing in my eyes or my ears, sit in silence and let a thought creep in every once in a while.</p>

<p>My default whenever I am even slightly bored is to quell that boredom by any means necessary. But when I was a kid, boredom was when I would write endless stories in my head, sing little melodies, and let my imagination run free. Like those field recordings that mark the quieting of birdsongs as various factors silence the sound of biodiversity, my own head has become a digital wasteland with soundbytes and tiktok audios ringing ad nauseum until I sleep. I must allow silence to return, maybe then so will the birdsongs.</p>

<h1 style="color: #F2928E">5. What we imagine, we can become</h1>

<p style="color: #F2928E">My favorite quote from Braiding Sweetgrass is when Kimmerer states, “what we imagine, we can become.” Since my very first undergraduate geography course, I’ve always been captivated and invigorated by the idea of collective imagination. It can initially seem frivolous. Imagination is literally defined as “not present to the senses.” It's far from tangible until you realize that everything we have in our current world was once imagined and subsequently implemented. The more we think about concepts of border, gender, race––infrastructure we socially accept as concrete––the more imaginary they appear to be. The line between real and imagined no longer holds as fact.</p>

<p style="color: #F2928E">As AI is increasingly trained to mimic human imagination and the ability to “create,” it remains vital to remember that its mock ability only even exists because of our true intrinsic nature to imagine. Our imaginaries precede AI, and its making is reliant on us. While much of this notion can lean towards devastation, it’s crucial to remember that we can envision a better world. Our imaginaries are boundless and the one sure thing that ensures and fortifies our existence as living beings. If we all imagine what a better world could look like, I am hopeful we can one day live that truth.</p>
